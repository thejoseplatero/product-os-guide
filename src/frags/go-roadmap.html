GO Product Roadmap

TLDR: Build Roman Pichler's five-row goal-oriented roadmap end to end, with his official checklist applied as hard gates before anything counts as done. Reach for it when actually constructing or reviewing a GO roadmap, making an internal versus public version, or converting a feature-date roadmap into a goal-led one.

The full idea

The source is Roman Pichler's GO Product Roadmap template and checklist, version 01 from 2023, at romanpichler.com. Roadmapping picks a format. This skill builds one. It applies Pichler's checklist as build gates, not as an afterwards review.

One column per slice, a release or a period. Five rows per slice. Date: when the goal should be met, a timeframe, not a promise. Name: memorable and fitting. Goal: the outcome or benefit, exactly one per slice. Features: three to five high-level capabilities needed to meet the goal. Metrics: how you will know, precise and time-bound. The defining rule: the goal is the commitment, the features are trimmable examples. If a negotiation is about a feature, redirect it to the goal.

The builder has three modes. Build, from a strategy input. Review, which lists gate failures on an existing roadmap, most damaging first, with fixes. And Bootstrap, when you have only an idea. Bootstrap runs a seven-question interview to produce a minimal Lean Canvas: who, what problem, why now, solution as a hypothesis, what is different, how you will know, how it makes money. Then one vision line: in five years, what is different in the world?

Build order is the heart of it, and it is never features first. Goals first: three to five per horizon, each traceable to a need, sequenced by dependency and cost of delay. Metrics second: how will you measure it and when, like "one week after release." Features last, three to five, only what is crucial. Name and date once the slice has substance. Features first with goals captioned on afterwards is a feature Gantt in disguise. Rebuild it, do not relabel it.

Every goal must pass five gates. Outcome-based: it states value created, never an activity. Specific: enough detail to tell what must be done. Measurable: a target you can verify. Prioritised: placed with dependencies and cost of delay in mind. Single: one goal per slice. The whole roadmap must be Connected, Adaptive with a quarterly review, Shared, and Actionable with no speculative slices.

Here is a slice from our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Date: Q4. Name: Beat the Clock. Goal: win back the short-break lunch customer so that lunch-window order completion rises 8 points. Metrics: lunch-window completion measured four weeks after release, against the pre-launch baseline. Features: arrival window shown on the list view, late-order credit, kitchen speed filter. Now watch a weak version fail. "Launch the lunch guarantee" is an activity, so it fails Outcome-based. "Improve lunch engagement" measured by "lunch engagement improves" fails Measurable; the metric restates the goal. Placeholder numbers like "[N] percent" fail on purpose. Commit to a number and a date.

One last rule: internal versions may carry dates like "Q1." Public versions get coarse timeframes like "first half of 2027," or drop the date row entirely. Never let the external version become the master. For a PM, the gates matter because a roadmap with open failures is a draft, and a draft shown as a roadmap is a promise you did not mean to make.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is a GO roadmap?

15 min
Search
Explain Roman Pichler's GO Product Roadmap in simple English: the five rows, the one-goal-per-slice rule, and why the goal is the commitment while features are trimmable. Name the five goal gates and the four overall gates from his checklist.
Get
The five rows, the defining rule, and the nine gates listed.
Build
Take the Now column of the Now/Next/Later roadmap you built in Roadmapping and lay one item out as a five-row slice for the lunch gap. Label every row. Circle anything sitting in the goal row that is really a feature, and rewrite it as the outcome it is supposed to produce.
Capture
Write the five rows into your doc and the rule that separates a goal from a feature. A starting stem: "The goal is the commitment, which means features are..."
STEP 2

Bootstrap the strategy input

15 min
Search
Pretend I have no strategy at all for closing the weekday lunch gap. Run the Bootstrap interview: seven questions, one at a time, push back on vague answers, then play back a minimal Lean Canvas with a five-year vision line and ask me to confirm.
Get
A confirmed minimal Lean Canvas: customer, problem, solution hypothesis, UVP, key metrics, revenue, vision line.
Build
Run the interview cold. Answer question one with "everyone who eats lunch," read the push-back you get, then answer again with one real person from the personas you built in Personas and Archetypes. When it plays the canvas back, hold it next to the Lean Canvas you filled in Business Canvases and mark every box where the two disagree. Those gaps are where your strategy is still soft.
Capture
Write into your doc what a goal needs behind it before you will write it down. A starting stem: "I never write a goal without..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the five-row format, your build order, the goal and overall gates, your bootstrap questions, and your internal versus public rule.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named go-roadmap. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Write a lunch gap roadmap where every slice lists features and the goals were written afterwards, and ask Claude: "use my go-roadmap skill to review this and rebuild it goal-first".

Smells to watch for

  • A slice with more than one goal (it aligns nobody).
  • A metric that restates the goal ("improve engagement" measured by "engagement improves").
  • Features filled in first with goals captioned on afterwards: a feature Gantt in disguise.
  • Placeholder numbers like "[N] percent" left in the metrics row.
  • The public version with dates becoming the master roadmap.
