JTBD Framing

TLDR: Jobs-to-be-done turns a feature request or a vague idea into the real job a customer is trying to get done, the moment they struggle, and what they compare you against. Reach for it when you evaluate a feature idea, write a problem statement, or need to challenge someone's pet feature.

The full idea

This idea comes from Bob Moesta and Clayton Christensen. Their claim is simple: people do not buy products, they hire them. They hire a product to make progress in a specific situation. So the thing we study is not the persona and not the feature. It is the struggling moment: the exact situation where someone is stuck and wants to move forward.

The method has four steps. First, find the struggling moment. Write it as "When [specific situation], I struggle to [the progress I want]." If you cannot picture the situation clearly, you do not have a job yet. You have a category. Second, write the job statement: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Here is the test: delete your product name. Does the sentence still make sense? If the statement mentions your feature, it is circular and you need to start over.

Third, map the four forces. There is the push of the current situation, meaning what hurts right now. There is the pull of the new solution, meaning what looks attractive. Then there is anxiety about the new thing, and habit, which is everything that keeps people where they are. Most teams only work on pull. But a feature that adds pull and ignores anxiety and habit loses to "do nothing," and "do nothing" is the most hired competitor in every market.

Fourth, name the real competition. What do people hire today for this job? Often it is a spreadsheet, a phone call, or simply ignoring the problem. That is the bar you have to beat, not the big company in your category.

Take our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. A stakeholder looks at that number and says "we need a lunch promo." That is a feature wearing a trench coat. Ask for the struggling moment instead: "When I get 40 minutes for lunch on a work day, I struggle to get real food without losing half my break to waiting." Now the job is clear. The push is a short break and a boring desk drawer. The pull is hot food showing up on time. The anxiety is "what if it arrives at 1:20 and I eat cold noodles in a meeting." The habit is the sandwich shop downstairs and the lunch packed at home, which are fast, cheap, and never late. The real competitor is the sandwich shop, not the other delivery app.

The most common mistake is letting personas do the work that circumstances should do. "Millennials want convenience" tells you nothing. The same person hires a different product at noon on a work day and at 9pm on a Sunday. Circumstance, not demographics. This matters to a PM because every roadmap decision is a bet on a job. If nobody is struggling, nothing gets hired, no matter how good the demo looks.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is a job to be done?

15 min
Search
Explain jobs-to-be-done as Bob Moesta and Clayton Christensen describe it. What is a struggling moment, and why is it a better unit of analysis than a persona or a feature? Give one example using this case: Uber Eats is growing, but weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm are 30% below dinner.
Get
A one-line definition of a job, a clear idea of what a struggling moment is, and why the same person hires different products in different circumstances.
Build

Capture
Write your own definition of a job and your test for a real struggling moment into your doc, one or two sentences each. A starting stem if it helps: "A struggling moment is specific enough when..."
STEP 2

Write the job statement

20 min
Search
Take this observation: Uber Eats weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm are 30% below dinner. Write the weekday lunch job as a statement in the form: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. Then delete the product name and check the statement still works.
Get
A job statement that survives with the product name removed, plus the rule for spotting a circular statement.
Build
Write your own job statement for the lunch gap, grounded in the strongest quote from your Customer Interviews notes. Delete "Uber Eats" from it and check the sentence still stands. If it collapses, it was circular. Rewrite it until it survives.
Capture
Write the job statement template into your doc in your own words, plus the one-line rule for when a statement fails. Then write your lunch gap job statement underneath it. This statement feeds almost every later guide.
STEP 3

Map the four forces

20 min
Search
For the job "when I get a short lunch break on a work day, I want hot food to arrive before my break ends, so I can eat without stress," map the four forces: push, pull, anxiety, habit. Be concrete. Then tell me which two forces most teams ignore and why that loses to "do nothing."
Get
Four short lists, one per force, and a clear understanding that anxiety and habit are the forces that keep people from switching.
Build
On paper, draw the four forces for the job statement you just wrote. Pull the pushes and anxieties from your Customer Interviews quotes. Add at least three anxieties and three habits. Count how many of them a "nice feature" alone would not fix.
Capture
Write the four forces into your doc with one lunch gap example each, then add a rule in your own words about which forces a feature alone cannot beat. A starting stem: "A feature that only adds pull loses to..."
STEP 4

Name the real competition

15 min
Search
List what office workers actually hire today for the weekday lunch job, including non-app options like a packed lunch, the sandwich shop downstairs, the office cafeteria, or skipping lunch. Explain why these are the real bar for Uber Eats at lunchtime, not the rival delivery app.
Get
A list of real alternatives, including "do nothing," and the habit of asking "what do they use today?" before "what does the competitor have?"
Build
Go back through your Customer Interviews notes and list every lunch alternative your interviewees mentioned. Tally how many involve no app at all. If the notes are thin, ask Claude to play a desk-bound office worker and ask it what it actually did for lunch every day last week.
Capture
Write your competition rule into your doc, plus the ranked list of what the lunch gap customer hires today. A starting stem: "Before I name a competitor, I ask..."
STEP 5

Work the four dimensions of the job

25 min
Search
Explain the functional, emotional, personal, and social dimensions of a job to be done, in simple English with one example of each. Why do teams almost always find the functional dimension and miss the other three?
Get
Four readings of the same job. Functional is the practical, objective requirement: get fed inside the break. Emotional is how the person wants to feel while doing it: unbothered, not anxious about the clock. Personal is how they feel about the solution itself: competent rather than wasteful or lazy. Social is how they believe others see them using it: the person who has it together, or the one whose lunch turns up late to a meeting. Teams find the functional dimension because it is the one people volunteer, and miss the other three because nobody says them out loud.
Build
Take your lunch gap job statement and write it out four times, once per dimension. Then go back to the interview evidence from Customer Interviews and mark which dimension each quote actually belongs to. If every quote lands in the functional column, that is a finding about your interview script, not about your customers, so write the two follow-up questions that would get at the emotional and social readings.
Capture
Write the four dimensions of your lunch gap job into your doc. Then add one sentence that starts "The dimension we were least likely to have designed for is ..." and say what that would change.
STEP 6

Separate the main job from the related jobs

20 min
Search
What is the difference between a main job to be done and a related job? Why does a product that nails the main job and ignores the related jobs still lose? Give an example.
Get
The main job is the goal the customer is hiring you for. Related jobs are what they need to accomplish alongside it, in the same episode, for the main job to count as done. Getting fed is the main job. Deciding fast without a scroll, splitting the bill with three colleagues, and knowing when it will land so a meeting can be scheduled around it are related jobs. A product can nail the main job and still lose, because the related job is where the person actually gives up.
Build
Write your lunch gap main job at the top of a page. Underneath, list every related job in the same episode, from the moment hunger starts to the moment the person is back at their desk. Use the journey you will map later if you have it, or just walk the episode minute by minute now. Mark which related jobs are currently served well, badly, or not at all.
Capture
Write the main job and the related jobs into your doc, with the badly served ones marked. Then add one sentence that starts "The related job most likely to be killing the main one is ..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds a definition of a job, the job statement template with its delete-the-product test, the four forces, and the rule for naming real competition, all worked on the lunch gap.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named jtbd-framing. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Take the feature request "add a lunch deals carousel to the home screen" and ask Claude: "use my jtbd-framing skill to turn this feature request into a job statement and four forces for the lunch gap."

Smells to watch for

  • A job statement that is really a feature request in disguise, like "I want to export CSV so I can have CSV."
  • Personas doing the work that circumstances should do, like "millennials want..."
  • No named struggling moment. If nobody is struggling, nothing gets hired.
  • A job statement that stops making sense when you delete the product name.
