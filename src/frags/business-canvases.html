Business Canvases

TLDR: A canvas puts a whole business model on one page so contradictions cannot hide, and every box is a guess with a confidence level. Reach for it when shaping a new product, stress-testing an existing model, or turning a 30-page business case into one honest page.

The full idea

Alexander Osterwalder gave us the Business Model Canvas and the Value Proposition Canvas in Business Model Generation. Ash Maurya gave us the Lean Canvas in Running Lean. The idea is the same: a canvas is a shared argument on one page, so contradictions cannot hide in separate chapters. And a canvas is a set of guesses. Treat every box as a hypothesis with a confidence level, not a fact.

Choosing the canvas. The Lean Canvas is Maurya's startup remix of Osterwalder's original, and it is the one this case needs. It swaps partners, relationships, and resources for Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, and Unfair Advantage. Use it when the question is "is there a business here at all?" for a specific segment you have not served properly yet. The swapped-in boxes are the ones a team is most likely to lie to itself about. The Business Model Canvas is for an operating business or a corporate setting: how the whole machine makes money, including partners, channels, and cost structure. The Value Proposition Canvas is a zoom lens on one segment: customer jobs, pains, and gains against your pain relievers and gain creators. Use it when the value box is hand-waving.

The Lean Canvas does not start from a blank page here. It eats what discovery already produced. The Segment box takes the personas you built in Personas and Archetypes. The Problem box takes the job statement from JTBD Framing and the top opportunities from your opportunity solution tree. The Value box takes the value statements from Value Proposition. If those boxes disagree with each other on one page, that disagreement is the finding.

Fill it honestly, and the order matters. Start on the customer side, never the product side: segment, then problem and jobs, then alternatives, then value, then channel, then revenue. Only then the delivery machinery. A canvas filled from "our solution" outward produces a brochure. Rules per box. Problem: top three, with existing alternatives named, and "nothing" is a valid and deadly alternative. Segment: narrow enough to find ten this week. Unfair advantage: something that cannot be easily copied or bought, and most first drafts here are blank. Key metrics: one or two. Revenue and costs: coarse math is fine, no math is not. One multiplication, price times plausible buyers, exposes most fantasy models.

Our case on a Lean Canvas. Uber Eats grows overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Segment: office workers with a fixed break under 45 minutes, in buildings without a cafeteria. Problem: a wide delivery estimate at noon means gambling the whole break, so people walk to the sandwich shop instead. Alternatives: the sandwich shop downstairs, a lunch packed at home, and skipping lunch. Value: food that arrives inside a window you can plan a meeting around. Key metric: weekday lunch orders per active customer in the 11:30 to 1:30 window. Revenue: coarse math on how many lunch orders one 30% recovery would be worth per city per week. Unfair advantage: blank for now, and that blank is the most useful thing on the page.

Then mark each box green, yellow, or red by evidence strength. The red boxes are your discovery backlog. Rank them by "what kills the business fastest if wrong," Maurya's riskiest-assumption order, and send the top one into the four-risks skill for its cheapest test. Re-canvas at every material learning. Keeping the old versions is the cheapest strategy journal you will ever have.

The most common mistake is a canvas with every box confidently full. No reds means no honesty, not no risk. This matters to a PM because executives will ask "does the business work?" and one page they can attack beats thirty they will not read.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is a business canvas?

15 min
Search
Explain the Lean Canvas by Ash Maurya, the Business Model Canvas by Osterwalder, and the Value Proposition Canvas. What is each one for, and how do I choose between them? Why is the Lean Canvas the right one for a question like this: Uber Eats weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm run 30% below dinner, and nobody has served that lunch segment on purpose yet. Why should every box be treated as a hypothesis?
Get
Three canvases, what question each answers, why the Lean Canvas leads here, and the rule that every box is a guess with a confidence level.
Build
Write one line for each canvas saying what question it would answer about the lunch gap, then pick the one you will actually fill and say why in one sentence. Then check what you already have: name which discovery artifact feeds each of the Segment, Problem, and Value boxes. If a box has nothing feeding it, mark it now as a guess.
Capture
Write your rule for choosing between the three canvases into your doc, plus the line about every box being a hypothesis. A starting stem: "I reach for the Lean Canvas when the question is..."
STEP 2

Fill it customer-side first

25 min
Search
Fill a Lean Canvas for the lunch gap: Uber Eats weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm run 30% below dinner, aimed at office workers with a break under 45 minutes. Go in this order: segment, problem with existing alternatives, value, channel, revenue, then solution, metrics, costs, unfair advantage. Keep the segment narrow enough to find ten this week. Keep one or two metrics only.
Get
A full Lean Canvas built in customer-first order, with a narrow segment, named alternatives, and one or two metrics.
Build
Fill a Lean Canvas for the lunch gap on paper, with the Solution box covered by a sticky note until the customer side is done. Put the two personas you built in Personas and Archetypes into the Segment box and narrow to whichever one you could find ten of this week. Put the job statement from JTBD Framing into the Problem box, with the real alternatives you listed there, including doing nothing. Put your Value Proposition statements into the Value box word for word. Then uncover the Solution box and ask whether the solution you already had in mind still fits the problem you just wrote.
Capture
Write your fill order into your doc, in your own words, plus what happens to a canvas filled from the solution outward. A starting stem: "I fill the canvas starting from..."
STEP 3

Do the one multiplication and test the unfair advantage

15 min
Search
For the lunch gap canvas, do one honest revenue multiplication: plausible lunch orders recovered times margin per order. Then look at the unfair advantage box. Apply the benefit-and-barrier test and tell me whether "our brand," "first mover," or "AI" would survive. If nothing survives, leave the box blank and explain why that is useful.
Get
One multiplication with real numbers, and an unfair advantage box that is either a real barrier or honestly blank.
Build
Write the lunch gap multiplication out in full, in one line, starting from the 30% shortfall in the case observation. Say out loud where your "plausible buyers" number came from and whether you could defend it to a skeptic. Then write whatever you were tempted to put in the unfair advantage box and run the benefit-and-barrier test on it. If it does not survive, leave the box blank and write one line about why that blank is now the most honest thing on your page.
Capture
Write your revenue rule and your unfair advantage test into your doc, one sentence each. A starting stem: "An unfair advantage is real only if a competitor cannot..."
STEP 4

Color the boxes and build the discovery backlog

15 min
Search
Mark each box of the lunch gap canvas green, yellow, or red by evidence strength. Rank the red boxes by what kills the business fastest if wrong. Tell me which one to test first and what the cheapest test is. Explain why a canvas with no red boxes is a warning sign.
Get
A colored canvas, a ranked list of reds, and the rule that reds are the discovery backlog.
Build
Color every box on your lunch gap canvas with three markers. Green only where you can point at a specific interview quote or the case number itself. If you end up with zero reds, write down why you are so sure, and notice that you probably are not. Rank the reds by what kills this fastest if wrong, and carry the top one forward into Four Risks.
Capture
Write your coloring rule and what the red boxes become into your doc, plus the top red box on your lunch gap canvas by name. A starting stem: "A box is green only when..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the three canvases and how to choose, the customer-first fill order with per-box rules, the one multiplication, the unfair advantage test, and the green-yellow-red step that turns the canvas into a discovery backlog.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named business-canvases. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Write a two-paragraph pitch for a weekday lunch pass that closes the lunch gap, and ask Claude: "use my business-canvases skill to put this on a Lean Canvas and show me the red boxes."

Smells to watch for

  • A canvas filled in one sitting by one person and never touched again. That is a form, not a model.
  • Every box confidently full. No reds means no honesty, not no risk.
  • Unfair advantage written as "first mover," "our team," or "AI."
  • A value proposition written in the product's vocabulary instead of the segment's.
  • Twelve customer segments on one canvas. A canvas per segment beats a segment salad.
