Scoping and the MVP

TLDR: An MVP is the smallest product that tests your bet end to end, cut with a fixed time budget and a written not-doing list. Reach for it when a winning idea needs to become a buildable first slice instead of a wish for "phase 1".

The full idea

You now have a winning lunch gap bet from your prioritized cut. The next danger is the word "just": we will just build it. Scoping is the discipline that stands between the winner and a six-month build. Four ideas do the work.

First, Eric Ries and the minimum viable product. The words matter. Minimum: the smallest thing. Viable: it must actually work for a real user. Product: not a mockup, the thing itself. And the purpose is learning. An MVP is not version one with fewer features. It is the cheapest product that tests whether your bet is true. If your bet is "pre-order at 10am gets desk workers to order lunch," the MVP is whatever proves or kills that sentence fastest.

Second, the walking skeleton, which you will meet again in Story Mapping. The first slice must run the whole journey thinly: a user hears about pre-order, places one at 10am, gets food at 12:00, and comes back or does not. Every step exists, badly. Duct tape everywhere invisible. A beautiful ordering screen with no delivery behind it is not a skeleton, it is a severed arm.

Third, cupcake, not dry cake. If the vision is a wedding cake, the wrong MVP is a layer of plain sponge: a fragment of the final thing, complete in nothing. The right MVP is a cupcake: tiny, but it has cake, frosting, and delight in one bite. Small and whole beats large and partial. The pre-order MVP might serve one office district, three restaurants, and a single 12:00 delivery window. Tiny, complete, tastes like the real product.

Fourth, appetite, from Ryan Singer's Shape Up. Instead of asking "how long will this take?", you declare how much time it is worth: "this bet gets six weeks, no more." Time is fixed, scope is the variable. When the work bulges past the appetite, you scope hammer: swing at every piece and ask "does the bet still get tested without this?" Account creation? Order as a guest. Payment integration? Charge the saved card, or take payment on delivery. Ratings? Later. The hammer produces the most underrated artifact in product: the not-doing list. Written, explicit, with reasons. Scope you cut silently crawls back in the dark. Scope you cut in writing stays cut.

The common mistake is scoping by feature completeness instead of by learning. "It needs accounts, payments, and notifications to be real" is the dry cake talking. The only question is what the bet needs to be tested end to end, once, by real lunch orders. Everything else goes on the list you are proud to not be doing.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is an MVP?

15 min
Search
Explain Eric Ries's minimum viable product as a tool for learning, the walking skeleton, the cupcake versus dry cake way of slicing, and Shape Up's appetite (fixed time, variable scope) in simple English, with one food delivery example for each.
Get
Four ideas in one line each: MVP tests a bet, the skeleton walks the whole journey thinly, a slice is a cupcake not a sponge layer, and time is fixed while scope flexes.
Build
Take the winning bet from the cut you made in Innovation Games, say the 10am pre-order for 12:00 delivery, and write it as one testable sentence: who does what, and what result proves the bet.
Capture
Write the bet sentence into your doc, plus one sentence that starts "My MVP exists to learn whether ...".
STEP 2

Set the appetite

15 min
Search
My bet is [paste your bet sentence]. Help me set an appetite for it in the Shape Up sense: not an estimate of how long it takes, but a decision about how much time it is worth given the evidence behind it. Argue for two weeks, then argue for six weeks, then tell me what each appetite would force me to cut.
Get
An appetite you chose, with the reasoning, and a first feel for what that budget makes impossible.
Build
Check the appetite against your four risks assessment from Four Risks. A bet whose value risk is still high deserves a small appetite: you are buying learning, not building the answer. Pick the number and commit to it in writing.
Capture
Write one sentence that starts "This bet is worth ... weeks because ...", and treat it as fixed from here on.
STEP 3

Cut the slice

25 min
Search
Here is my bet and my appetite: [paste]. Help me define the walking skeleton: the thinnest end-to-end slice where one real user completes the whole pre-order journey once, from seeing the 10am offer to food at their desk at 12:00. List every step the journey needs, then propose the crudest acceptable version of each step. Then check it is a cupcake, not a sponge layer: small in reach, complete in journey.
Get
A slice defined step by step, with the crude version of each step named: maybe one office district, three restaurants, one delivery window, guest checkout, a manually sent confirmation.
Build
Walk the slice against the journey map you built in Journey Mapping and mark any journey step the slice skips entirely. A skipped step means the skeleton does not walk. Add the crudest version of that step rather than fattening any other step.
Capture
Write the slice into your doc as a numbered journey, one line per step, each line naming its crude version.
STEP 4

Scope hammer and write the not-doing list

20 min
Search
Scope hammer this slice against my appetite: [paste the slice and the appetite]. Go piece by piece and ask whether the bet still gets tested without it. Be aggressive. Then write the not-doing list: everything cut or deferred, with one reason each. Include the features that lost in my prioritized cut, like the ones that came out indifferent in my Kano results.
Get
A hammered slice that fits the appetite, and an explicit not-doing list with reasons: no accounts, no ratings, no second delivery window, no lunch subscription yet.
Build
Read the hammered slice one last time and try to break it: can one office worker still complete the whole journey and can you still measure whether the bet held? If yes to both, the hammer went exactly far enough.
Capture
Write the not-doing list into your doc next to the slice, and one sentence that starts "Scope comes back on the list only when ...". Both feed the PRD in the next phase.

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds your bet sentence, your appetite rule, your walking skeleton slice, and your not-doing list with reasons.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named scoping-mvp. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Ask Claude: "use my scoping-mvp skill to scope the runner-up from my lunch gap cut, the group order feature, with a two-week appetite" and check that it produces a slice and a not-doing list, not a feature list.

Smells to watch for

  • An MVP defined as "phase 1 features" instead of a thin end-to-end journey.
  • A slice that covers three steps of the journey completely and the rest not at all: a whole product to nobody.
  • Asking "how long will it take?" instead of deciding what the bet is worth.
  • Scope cut in conversation but never written down, which means it was never cut.
  • An MVP that could succeed or fail without teaching you anything about the bet.
