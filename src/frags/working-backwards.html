Working Backwards

TLDR: Write the press release and the FAQ before you build anything, so you find out whether the product is compelling while it still costs only a rewrite. Reach for it when kicking off a significant initiative, forcing clarity on a fuzzy vision, or replacing a 40-slide kickoff deck.

The full idea

This is Amazon's PR/FAQ process, described by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr in Working Backwards. Start from the customer's moment of launch and reason backwards. If the press release is not compelling, the product will not be either, and you just saved two quarters finding out.

The press release is one page, in customer language only. Six parts. A heading: the product name and the customer it serves. A subheading: one sentence with the benefit, not the mechanism. A problem paragraph: the customer's pain, written so the customer would say "yes, that is me." Write this one first and hardest. A solution paragraph: what they can now do, in plain words. If it needs jargon to sound good, it is not good yet. A customer quote: invented but plausible. If every quote sounds like marketing, the value is not concrete enough. And how to start: the first run in two sentences. Rules: no superlatives, no internal language, no feature lists.

The FAQ is where the honesty lives, in two halves. Customer FAQs are the skeptical buyer's questions: price, migration, what happens to my data, why not the alternative. Internal FAQs are the hard questions leadership will ask: unit economics, why now, why us, biggest technical risk, what we are not doing, what kills this. An internal FAQ with no uncomfortable question in it means the team is hiding the body. Every one of the four risks should appear as a question with a real answer or an honest "we do not know yet, here is the test."

Take our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Heading: "Noon: lunch that beats your break." Subheading: "See one arrival time you can trust before you pay, or the lunch is on us." Problem paragraph: "At 11:40 every weekday, Priya has 40 minutes and no plan. The app says her food lands somewhere between 12:35 and 1:05. Her break ends at 12:50. She closes the app and stands in the sandwich line for 18 minutes instead, holding her phone." Solution: "Priya opens the app at 11:40 and sees one time, 12:18, from a kitchen that can hold that slot. She pays. It arrives at 12:16." Quote: "I stopped ordering lunch because I could not risk it. Now I know when it lands," said Priya, who works in a tower downtown. Internal FAQ: "What does a missed window cost us per order, and how many can we afford?" "What share of kitchens can actually hold a noon slot on a busy day?" If you cannot answer those, you learned something before writing code.

How it is used: written by the owner, read silently in the room, not presented, because reading cannot be carried by charisma. Then attacked. Each round of "would a customer care about this sentence?" is discovery at the cost of a rewrite. The version that survives becomes the north-star document, and every scope cut is tested against "does the press release still hold?"

The most common mistake is a press release written after the roadmap commitment. That is backwards working backwards. This matters to a PM because it is the cheapest way to find out your idea is boring, and it forces every stakeholder to agree on what "done" looks like before anyone argues about how.

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the six-part press release with its rules, the problem-paragraph-first habit, the two-half FAQ with the uncomfortable-question test, and the silent-read, attack, and revise process.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named working-backwards. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Take the three-line idea "pre-order tomorrow's lunch at 9am so it lands at 12:15" as an answer to the lunch gap, and ask Claude: "use my working-backwards skill to write the press release and FAQ for this."

Smells to watch for

  • A press release written after the roadmap commitment. Backwards working backwards.
  • A customer quote no human would say aloud.
  • An FAQ where every answer is confident.
  • The mechanism starring in the headline instead of the outcome.
