Moats

TLDR: A moat is a condition that gives you lasting better returns: a benefit plus a barrier that stops competitors from copying it. Reach for it when asking "what stops a competitor from copying this," or when a strategy doc claims differentiation.

The full idea

This is Hamilton Helmer, from 7 Powers. He calls a moat a "power": a condition that creates persistent differential returns. The key is that a power needs two things at once, a benefit and a barrier. A benefit without a barrier is a feature head start: temporary, and copied by anyone it hurts.

The seven powers. Scale economies: unit cost falls with volume, and the barrier is the loss a challenger must eat to match your scale. Network economies: value rises with users, and the barrier is that a small copy of a network is worthless. Counter-positioning: a new model the incumbent will not copy because it would cannibalize their core. The most available power for a small player. Switching costs: leaving hurts because of data, integrations, workflow, retraining. Earned over usage, invisible at purchase. Branding: habitual trust that commands a premium. Cornered resource: exclusive access to a patent, a dataset, a team, a supply deal. Process power: an embedded way of working that rivals can see but not copy on any useful timeline.

Apply two tests to any claimed moat. Benefit: does it materially improve cash flow or customer value? Barrier: what specifically prevents a competent, funded competitor from copying it within the payback window? "We move faster" and "our UX is better" fail the second test. That is operating excellence, which Helmer rules out as power: necessary, insufficient, and imitable.

Stage matters too. Counter-positioning and cornered resource are available at the start. Network and switching costs compound during takeoff. Scale, brand, and process power arrive at maturity. The right question is "which power is this bet positioned to earn, and does the roadmap feed it?"

Take our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Someone says "our moat is the best app experience." Test two fails immediately. Now look for a real power. Lunch is not dinner: it is one narrow rush, in a few dense office districts, where the only thing that matters is whether food lands before the break ends. A bet: buy exclusive reserved noon prep slots from the two fastest kitchens in each office district, paying for the slot whether it fills or not. That is a cornered resource. Benefit: we can promise an arrival time nobody else can promise, because the kitchen is not queueing our order behind forty others. Barrier: there is only one noon, and the slot is already sold. The barrier becomes real the day the fastest kitchens in a district are all booked. What does a rational competitor do in the meantime? Pay those kitchens more to break the deal. So the roadmap has to feed the power: make the noon-hour tools good enough that a kitchen would not leave even for money.

For any bet, name one intended power, not a menu, with the benefit and barrier in one sentence each. If no power is nameable, the bet may still be right, but it is a race, and you should price and pace it like one. The most common mistake is "our moat is AI." A supplier's product is nobody's cornered resource. This matters to a PM because "defensible" is the word that separates a feature from a business.

Why does he rule out operating excellence as a power? Use this case: Uber Eats weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm run 30% below dinner.

Build

Capture

Build
Write your own lunch gap example for each of the seven powers, three lines each: the benefit, the barrier, and the stage it becomes available. Capture

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the benefit-and-barrier definition, the seven powers with stages, the two tests, and the five-line format for applying a power to a bet.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named moats. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Write a strategy paragraph claiming "our AI lunch picks and fast delivery are what will close the lunch gap" and ask Claude: "use my moats skill to pressure-test these claims and name the real power, if any."

Smells to watch for

  • "Our moat is AI." A supplier's product is nobody's cornered resource.
  • Moat claims that are adjectives: faster, simpler, better.
  • First-mover advantage cited alone. Being early is a schedule, not a barrier.
  • Five powers claimed at once. Real strategies know which one they are building.
