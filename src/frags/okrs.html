OKRs

TLDR: Objectives and Key Results are a focusing device: one inspiring direction, two to four numbers that prove you arrived. Reach for it when drafting quarterly goals, converting output goals to outcome goals, or when OKRs have become a task list with percentages.

The full idea

OKRs come from Andy Grove at Intel, written up in High Output Management. John Doerr carried them to Google and wrote Measure What Matters. Christina Wodtke added the weekly rhythm in Radical Focus. Here is the core idea. An OKR is a focusing device. The objective is the inspiring direction. The key results are how you will know you arrived. And its real power is what it makes you not do.

Writing the objective: it is qualitative, memorable, time-bound, and slightly uncomfortable. One line a team member could recite cold. If it could be any team's objective, like "delight our customers," it is fluff. Go back to your strategy first. Writing the key results: two to four per objective, and each one is an outcome with a number, never an activity. "Ship the lunch arrival window" is an output. You can ship it while making everything worse. "Weekday lunch orders per active customer from 0.7 to 1.0" is an outcome. It is the reason the window exists.

The conversion test is simple. For any activity-shaped key result, ask "so that what?" until you hit a number in the world. Then write that number. Also attach the counter-metric from your metrics tree, or the key result is gameable by definition.

Let us try it on our case. Uber Eats grows overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Objective: "Make lunch a decision nobody has to gamble on." Key results: weekday lunch orders per active customer between 11:30 and 1:30 goes from 0.7 to 1.0. Time from opening the app at noon to placing the order drops under 60 seconds. Counter-metric: the share of lunch orders that arrive inside the promised window stays at or above today's level, so nobody buys speed by widening the promise. Notice there is no feature in there. The team can build the arrival window, or fix the noon capacity model, or do something nobody has thought of yet.

Now the cascade. Company OKRs set what matters. Teams draft their own OKRs answering "how does our lever move that," and roughly half should be proposed bottom-up. This is Grove's point: people commit to goals they authored. Leadership's review job is coherence checking. Do these team key results plausibly add up to the company one? It is not assignment. OKRs handed down whole become compliance theater by week three.

Grading and cadence. Aspirational key results target around 70 percent attainment, so a straight-A quarter means the targets were sandbagged, and you should say so, gently. Committed key results, like contracts, compliance, or dates promised outside the company, target 100 percent and get labeled differently. Mixing the two silently is how "stretch goal" becomes an excuse. Set quarterly. Check confidence weekly: Wodtke's Monday ritual, per key result, is confidence up or down from last week and why. Grade at quarter end in one honest hour. A key result rolled over twice without examination is a strategy question wearing a metrics costume. The most common mistake is the roadmap with percent signs. If your key results list features, you have a plan, not a goal. As a PM, OKRs are how you say no to a hundred good ideas and mean it.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is an OKR?

15 min
Search
Explain OKRs as Andy Grove and John Doerr describe them, in simple English. What makes a good objective, what makes a key result an outcome instead of an output, and why is an OKR called a focusing device?
Get
A definition of objective and key result, and the difference between an output and an outcome.
Build
Write one objective for the lunch gap quarter, taken straight from the guiding policy you wrote in Strategy Kernel, not from a wish. Read it back and check two things: could any team in any company have written it, and could a teammate repeat it from memory. Then write three draft key results fast, without editing, and mark each one output or outcome. Most first drafts are outputs, and seeing that is the point.
Capture
Write into your doc what makes an objective yours and what a key result must always be. A starting stem: "A key result is a number in the world, which means..."
STEP 2

Convert outputs with "so that what?"

20 min
Search
Here are my lunch gap key results. For each one that is an activity, ask me "so that what?" until we reach a number in the world. Then rewrite the key result as that number and attach a counter-metric.
Get
Two to four outcome key results per objective, each with a counter-metric.
Build
Take the outputs you marked in step 1 and run them through "so that what?" three times each. Start with "ship the guaranteed lunch arrival window" and keep asking until you land on a number that exists in your Metrics Trees tree. Rewrite each key result as that number, and attach the counter-metric you already paired it with in Metrics Trees. Cut until you have no more than four.
Capture
Write your conversion rule into your doc in one or two sentences, plus your finished lunch gap key results. A starting stem: "When a key result names an activity, I ask..."
STEP 3

Cascade without dictating

15 min
Search
The company objective is to make weekday lunch a decision nobody has to gamble on, closing the lunch gap. Help three teams (lunch checkout, courier dispatch, restaurant partners) each draft their own OKR that moves the company key result. Then check whether the team key results plausibly add up.
Get
Three team OKRs, at least half proposed by the teams, plus a coherence check.
Build
Take the three leaf owners you assigned on your Metrics Trees tree. Ask Claude to play each one in turn and draft its own OKR for the lunch gap quarter, arguing for what it thinks it can move. Then switch hats to leadership and ask one question only: does this add up to the company key result. Write down where it does not.
Capture
Write leadership's real job in an OKR review into your doc, in one sentence. A starting stem: "In an OKR review I am checking whether..."
STEP 4

Grade and run the weekly check

15 min
Search
Label each of my lunch gap key results as aspirational (target around 70 percent) or committed (target 100 percent). Then set up a Monday confidence check in Christina Wodtke's style: per key result, confidence up or down and why.
Get
Each key result labeled, plus a one-line weekly check template.
Build
Label each of your lunch gap key results aspirational or committed, and notice whether you quietly made everything committed so it would look safe. Then run week six as a rehearsal: your confidence on the lunch order key result has dropped, because the restaurants are slower at noon than the model assumed. Write the three sentences you would say on Monday and the one thing you would change. Ask Claude to play a leader who only wants to hear it is fine, and practise saying it anyway.
Capture
Write your grading rule and your weekly check into your doc. A starting stem: "A quarter where every key result hits 100 percent means..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds your objective test, your "so that what?" conversion rule, your cascade rule, and your grading and weekly cadence.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named okrs. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Write a set of lunch gap OKRs where every key result is a feature with a percent sign, and ask Claude: "use my okrs skill to convert these into outcome key results and tell me what to cut".

Smells to watch for

  • Key results that are the roadmap with percent signs.
  • Ten objectives (a focusing device pointed at everything focuses nothing).
  • 100 percent attainment across the board, celebrated.
  • OKRs written in week three of the quarter, graded never.
  • Individual-person OKRs for work that is really a team outcome.
