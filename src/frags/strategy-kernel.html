Strategy Kernel

TLDR: A strategy has three parts: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. Anything missing one of them is a wish, a slogan, or a budget. Reach for it when drafting or reviewing a strategy, or when a "strategy" is really a list of goals.

The full idea

This is Richard Rumelt, from Good Strategy Bad Strategy. His claim is that a strategy is a kernel with three parts, and if one is missing you do not have a strategy. You have a wish, a slogan, or a budget.

Part one is the diagnosis: what is actually going on, named plainly. A good diagnosis simplifies the overwhelming mess down to the critical aspects. "We lose enterprise deals at security review" beats "the market is competitive." The diagnosis is falsifiable and often uncomfortable. If everyone nods instantly, it is probably not a diagnosis.

Part two is the guiding policy: the overall approach you chose to deal with the diagnosis. The key word is chose, because a real policy excludes other approaches. A policy that forbids nothing guides nothing. That exclusion is the strategy.

Part three is coherent actions: resource commitments that reinforce each other and follow from the policy. The coherence test: if a stranger read only the list of actions, could they guess the policy? If the actions could belong to any strategy, they belong to none.

Let us do our case. Uber Eats grows overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Bad strategy: "Become the platform people love at lunch by delighting customers and closing the gap 30%." No diagnosis, no exclusion, a goal dressed as a plan. Good kernel. Diagnosis: "Weekday lunch buyers have a fixed break under 45 minutes, and our noon estimate is a wide range built for evening demand, so ordering lunch is a gamble against a meeting, and they take the certain sandwich instead." Guiding policy: "Win the weekday lunch hour by being the only option with a promise you can plan a meeting around, on a short list of restaurants that agreed to it, not by having the most lunch choices." That excludes the "run lunch discounts across the whole catalog" play. Coherent actions: sign noon prep-time agreements with a handful of restaurants per office district, stage couriers in those districts before 11:15, show a single arrival time instead of a range for that list only, and stop spending lunch marketing on breadth of selection. A stranger reading those four actions could guess the policy. That is coherence.

Rumelt also names four hallmarks of bad strategy, and you should call them out by name in any review. Fluff: inflated language that restates the obvious, like "customer-centric lunchtime excellence." Failure to face the challenge: a lunch strategy that never mentions the 45 minute break or the kitchen at noon. Mistaking goals for strategy: "close the lunch gap by 30%" is an ambition, not a how. Bad objectives: a long list of everything everyone wants, or objectives as impossible as the challenge they avoid.

The last idea is leverage. Strategy works by concentrating force where it matters: finding the pivot point where focused effort produces a disproportionate result. A strategy that spreads resources evenly across ten priorities has chosen not to have a pivot point. The most common mistake is the word "and" doing heavy lifting in the guiding policy, trying to keep every option open. This matters to a PM because your roadmap is only defensible if someone can trace every line of it back to a diagnosis and a policy that said no to something.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is a strategy kernel?

15 min
Search
Explain Richard Rumelt's strategy kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions. One plain sentence each. Then write a bad "strategy" for closing the lunch gap at Uber Eats (weekday lunch orders 11:30am to 1:30pm running 30% below dinner) that is really a goal list, and say which part is missing.
Get
The three parts in one line each, and the rule that a missing part means it is not a strategy.
Build
Write the sentence a stakeholder would say about the lunch gap: something like "we are going to own lunch." Mark which of the three parts it contains. Then look at what you already have and sort it: your Journey Mapping findings and Customer Interviews quotes are diagnosis material, your Prioritization ranking and kill list are action material, and you probably have nothing yet in the middle. That empty middle is the guiding policy.
Capture
Write the three parts into your doc in your own words, plus what a thing is when one part is missing. A starting stem: "Without a diagnosis, what I have is..."
STEP 2

Write a diagnosis that could be wrong

20 min
Search

Get
A diagnosis that names the real challenge and could be proven wrong, and the nod test.
Build

If you nod instantly and nothing in it makes you uneasy, it is a description, not a diagnosis, so rewrite it. Then write down what evidence would prove it wrong. If nothing could, rewrite it again.
Capture
Write your diagnosis test into your doc, plus your actual lunch gap diagnosis underneath it. A starting stem: "A diagnosis is doing its job when reading it makes me..."
STEP 3

Choose a policy that says no

20 min
Search
Given the diagnosis "weekday lunch buyers have a fixed break under 45 minutes and our noon estimate is a wide range, so ordering lunch is a gamble they refuse to take," write a guiding policy for Uber Eats. State clearly what the policy excludes. Then write four coherent actions and test whether a stranger could guess the policy from the actions alone.
Get
A guiding policy with a named exclusion, and a list of actions that pass the stranger test.
Build
Write one guiding policy for your lunch gap diagnosis, and under it write the sentence "this means we will not..." and finish it honestly. Then write four actions, pulling them from your Prioritization ranking so they are things you actually chose. Cover the policy, show Claude only the four actions, and ask it to guess the policy. If it cannot, your actions are not coherent, so change the actions rather than the policy.
Capture
Write your policy rule and your stranger test into your doc, plus the exclusion your lunch gap policy makes. A starting stem: "My guiding policy is only real if it forbids..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the three-part kernel, the nod test for a diagnosis, the exclusion rule for a policy, the stranger test for actions, and the four hallmarks of bad strategy.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named strategy-kernel. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Write a one-page "lunch strategy" that is really ten priorities and a target of closing the lunch gap by 30%, and ask Claude: "use my strategy-kernel skill to review this and rewrite it as a kernel."

Smells to watch for

  • The word "and" doing heavy lifting in the guiding policy, trying to keep every option.
  • A strategy doc with no sentence anyone would disagree with.
  • An actions list identical to last year's roadmap with new headers.
  • Ten "strategic priorities." Ten priorities is zero priorities.
