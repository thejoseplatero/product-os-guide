Briefs and Cases

TLDR: There is a ladder of documents below the PRD, from a half-page problem brief up to a business case, and each one is priced by the decision it serves. Reach for it when work needs a document but a PRD is too heavy, or when someone asks for "a quick business case" for something nobody has examined.

The full idea

This skill draws on Marty Cagan's opportunity assessment, Ryan Singer's Shape Up pitch with its idea of appetite, and the Amazon working-backwards discipline, whose PR/FAQ sits at the top of this ladder. The principle: documents are decision instruments, priced by the decision they serve. A two-week experiment documented like a capital request is theater. A quarter-long bet approved on a Slack message is gambling.

The ladder, bottom to top. Rung one, the problem brief, half a page. Decision: is this worth anyone's discovery time? Four parts: the struggling moment with quoted evidence, who has it and how often, what they do today, and why now. No solution content at all. Output: pursue or park.

Rung two, Cagan's opportunity assessment, one page. Decision: should a team spend discovery on this? Answer plainly: what problem, for whom, how big (one honest multiplication), what alternatives exist, why are we suited to win, why now, how will we measure success, what factors are critical, and a go or no-go. If three of eight answers are "we do not know," the output is a discovery plan, not a verdict. Say so instead of decorating the unknowns.

Rung three, the product brief or one-pager. Decision: alignment before detail. The PRD's trailer: problem, target user, the bet in one sentence, success as a number, what is out, open questions with owners. Its job is to get people disagreeing now, at one page, not at page nine of the PRD.

Rung four, the Shape Up pitch. Decision: should a team get a fixed appetite of time? Five parts: the problem with its raw story, the appetite ("worth six weeks, no more" instead of "how long will it take?"), a fat-marker solution, rabbit holes, and no-gos. Appetite is the strongest scope-control device on this ladder.

Rung five, the business case, two to three pages, only when real money moves. It needs a do-nothing baseline with its cost made explicit, build and run costs, benefits traced to an owned metric, risks, sensitivity, and kill criteria with a review date.

Take our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Someone says "write me a business case for fixing lunch." Ask what decision it serves. If it is "should one squad spend two weeks on discovery," that is a problem brief: "When my break starts I cannot tell if the food will beat the clock, so I go downstairs instead," quoted from three interviews, the lunch window is 30% below dinner and holds most weekday demand, people use the sandwich shop and the packed lunch today, and the noon rush is the only slot where our kitchens sit idle. Half a page. If it is "should we pay kitchens to hold reserved noon capacity across three districts," then yes, a business case, with the do-nothing cost spelled out.

The most common mistake is every initiative getting the same document regardless of size. That is ceremony, not judgment. This matters to a PM because matching the document to the decision's blast radius is how you earn the right to skip the paperwork when it does not matter and insist on it when it does.

Build it step by step

STEP 1

What is the document ladder?

15 min
Search
Explain the ladder of product documents below the PRD: problem brief, Cagan's opportunity assessment, product one-pager, Shape Up pitch, business case. For each, name the decision it serves and its length. Why should the document be priced by the decision, not by the org's habits? Use this case: Uber Eats weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm run 30% below dinner.
Get
Five rungs, each with its decision and length, and the principle of matching the document to the decision's blast radius.
Build
Write down five lunch gap requests of very different size: recolouring the lunch tab, promising an arrival time, opening a fourth office district, paying kitchens to hold noon capacity, and a weekly lunch subscription. Give each one a rung and one line defending it. Check the biggest one against the riskiest assumption in the hypothesis you wrote in Research and Hypothesis.
Capture
Write the five rungs into your doc and how you pick one. A starting stem: "I pick the rung by the size of the decision, which means..."
STEP 2

Write a problem brief with no solution

20 min
Search
Write a half-page problem brief for the weekday lunch gap. Four parts: the struggling moment with quoted evidence, who has it and how often, what they do today, why now. Include zero solution content. End with pursue or park.
Get
A half-page brief with quotes instead of paraphrase, the workaround named as the competitor, and no solution.
Build
Write your own brief using real quotes from your Customer Interviews notes, the job statement you wrote in JTBD Framing for the struggling moment, and the alternatives your interviewees named for "what they do today." Read it cold the next day, circle every sentence that hints at a solution, and delete each one.
Capture
Write the four parts of a problem brief into your doc and the one thing it never contains. A starting stem: "A problem brief contains no..."
STEP 3

Write an opportunity assessment and a one-pager

25 min
Search
Write Cagan's opportunity assessment for closing the weekday lunch gap: eight questions answered plainly, with one honest multiplication for size. Mark any answer that is really "we do not know." Then write the one-pager: problem, target user, the bet in one sentence, success as a number, what is out, open questions with owners.
Get
An assessment that admits its unknowns, and a one-pager built to collect disagreement.
Build
Answer all eight questions yourself, taking the size multiplication from the Lean Canvas you filled in Business Canvases and the target user from the personas you built in Personas and Archetypes. Mark every answer that is really a guess. Then write the one-pager and ask Claude to read it as a sceptical exec and give you three pushbacks. If it cannot find one, the one-pager has not said anything yet.
Capture
Write into your doc what happens when too many answers are unknown, and what a one-pager is for. A starting stem: "When most of the eight answers are unknown, the output is..."
STEP 4

Write a pitch with appetite, or a case with a do-nothing option

25 min
Search
Write a Shape Up pitch for closing the weekday lunch gap: problem with the raw story, appetite in weeks, solution at fat-marker level, rabbit holes, no-gos. Then, separately, outline a business case for paying kitchens to hold reserved noon capacity in three office districts, with a do-nothing baseline and its cost, build and run costs, benefits tied to an owned metric, sensitivity, and kill criteria with a review date.
Get
A pitch where appetite replaces the estimate, and a case where do-nothing is a real option with a cost.
Build
Write the no-gos for your own pitch, drawing the raw story straight from an interview quote. Then write the do-nothing cost for the case: what the lunch gap costs every week it stays open, as a number you can defend, not a feeling. Use the four risks you named in Four Risks as the risks section, and make one of them a kill criterion with a date.
Capture
Write what appetite means and what a business case always includes into your doc. A starting stem: "Appetite replaces the estimate, so instead of asking how long it takes I say..."

Make it a skill

Your doc now holds the five-rung ladder with the decision each serves, the no-solution rule for problem briefs, the eight assessment questions, the one-pager parts, the pitch with appetite and no-gos, and the business case with a do-nothing baseline.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named briefs-and-cases. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Take the request "write me a quick business case for a lunch deals carousel to close the lunch gap" and ask Claude: "use my briefs-and-cases skill to pick the right rung for this and write it."

Smells to watch for

  • Every initiative getting the same document regardless of size. Ceremony, not judgment.
  • An opportunity assessment where "why now" is "it is on the roadmap" or "AI."
  • A business case with no do-nothing option, the alternative that always exists.
  • A pitch with no rabbit holes or no-gos. Nothing shaped, everything pushed to the team.
  • The one-pager that is four pages.
