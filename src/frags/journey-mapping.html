Journey Mapping

TLDR: Mapping one person's end-to-end experience to find where it hurts, then using a service blueprint to find why it hurts backstage. Reach for it when problems span screens, channels, or teams, when onboarding or retention sags for unclear reasons, or before redesigning any multi-step flow.

The full idea

Three sources ground this skill. G. Lynn Shostack invented service blueprinting in the 1980s. Kerry Bodine and the Forrester lineage shaped modern journey mapping practice. And Daniel Kahneman's peak-end rule tells us how to read the emotional line. Hold onto this sentence: a journey map is a diagnostic instrument, not a poster. If it does not change a decision, it is wall art.

Scope it like a diagnostician. One persona, one goal, end to end, including the stages outside your product. The journey starts at the struggling moment, not at your login screen. "Priya, from the 11:40 stomach growl to having eaten at her desk" beats "the user experience of our app." The seams between channels, app to SMS to phone call to app again, are where journeys die, and no single team owns a seam by default. That is why the map has to.

Build the layers from evidence. Rows against a timeline of stages: doing, the observed actions; thinking and feeling, verbatims from research, not invented emotions; touchpoints per channel; and the emotional line. A map without research inputs is persona fan fiction. Read the line with peak-end in mind: the worst moment and the final moment dominate how people remember the whole thing. A mediocre middle matters less than one terrible peak or a limp ending. That reorders your fix list against intuition. Then mark the moments of truth: the two or three points where the relationship is decided. First value delivered, first failure handled, the loyalty ask.

When the cause is backstage, blueprint. The journey map shows where it hurts. The service blueprint shows why. Below the customer's actions you add frontstage, what they see; backstage, the staff and system actions they do not see; support processes; and the line of visibility. Most journey pain traces to something below that line.

Now our case. Uber Eats is growing overall, but weekday lunch orders between 11:30am and 1:30pm run 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Map one weekday lunch person. Priya opens the app at 11:47 with a meeting at 12:30. The estimate says 25 to 50 minutes, which is the whole question and no answer. She orders anyway at 11:52. The status sits on "Preparing" while the restaurant works a queue of twelve office orders at once. Food lands at 12:41, after the meeting started. Her verbatim: "I gambled my whole break on a range." The peak is the 12:30 mark passing with the status unchanged. The ending is eating cold noodles at 1:10. Above the line, the estimate screen looks fine. Below the line, lunch restaurants batch orders and the platform hands out the same wide estimate at noon that it uses at 8pm. Redesigning the estimate screen would be repainting a door that is nailed shut. The fix lives backstage: a lunch-hour capacity model and a narrower promise the kitchen has actually agreed to.

Output discipline: each mapped pain gets evidence, a stage, a severity, a suspected backstage cause, and an owner. Feed the top items into the roadmap. A map dated last year describes a product that no longer exists.

The most common mistake is building the map in a workshop from what the team assumes users feel. Why a PM cares: a gap like this rarely lives on one screen. The map is how you find the seam nobody owns, and then own it.

Make it a skill

Your doc now has the scope rule, the evidence-only layers, peak-end and moments of truth, the blueprint layers, and the output format with owners.

Turn this into a Claude skill file called SKILL.md named journey-mapping. Keep my words and my examples. Add the frontmatter with a name and a description that says when to use it. Do not add rules I did not write.

Then test it. Take six short transcripts from weekday lunch customers whose noon order arrived late and ask Claude: "use my journey-mapping skill to map the lunch gap journey and blueprint the worst pain".

Smells to watch for

  • Journey map built in a workshop from what the team assumes users feel.
  • Map that starts at the app open, when the decisive stages came before.
  • Emotions row with no verbatim behind any face.
  • Beautiful map, no owner per pain, nothing entered the roadmap.
  • Blueprint skipped because "we know why," while the backstage cause survives three frontstage redesigns.
