Customer Interviews

TLDR: Customer interviews done right produce evidence instead of politeness: questions about the past, a commitment test at the end, and synthesis within a day. Reach for it when writing an interview script, debriefing a call, or checking whether "validation" was real.

The full idea

Two sources here. Rob Fitzpatrick wrote The Mom Test, and Bob Moesta gave us the switch interview. Fitzpatrick's rule is the one to remember: even your mom cannot lie to you if you never mention your idea. People are polite about the future and honest about the past. So you ask about the past.

The Mom Test has three rules. One, talk about their life, not your idea. The moment the idea enters the room, the data leaves. Two, ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future. "Would you use X?" is worthless. "When did this last happen? Walk me through it" is gold. Three, talk less, listen more. You are mining for facts, workarounds, and money already spent. You are not pitching.

Some questions that work. "Tell me about the last time you had this problem." "What did you do about it?" The workaround is the real competitor and the real budget. "What have you already tried or paid for?" Spend is evidence, adjectives are not. "Who else struggles with this?" And the sharp one: "What happens if you just do not solve it?" If the answer is "nothing really," the pain does not clear the bar.

Let us run it on our case. Uber Eats is growing, but weekday lunch orders from 11:30am to 1:30pm sit 30% below dinner. Call it the lunch gap. Bad version: "Would you order lunch delivery more often if it were faster?" Everyone says yes. Good version: "Tell me about the last time you got lunch on a work day." Now you hear: "Tuesday, I had 35 minutes between meetings, I looked at the app, delivery said 40 to 55 minutes, so I grabbed a granola bar and kept working." That is a struggling moment, a workaround, and the cost of the workaround, all in one answer. Then: "What did you try?" "I pre-ordered once the night before. It showed up at 11:10, before I was hungry." Now you know timing, not appetite, is the fight.

Then comes the commitment test. Compliments are fool's gold. End every promising conversation with an ask that costs them something: their time (a follow-up session with their real order history), their reputation (an intro to a colleague who has the same problem), or their money (a deposit or a pilot fee). If they advance, that is real signal. "Sounds great, keep me posted" is a polite no. Log it as a no.

Moesta's switch interview is for real purchases. Find people who recently switched to or from a product and walk the timeline backwards: first thought, passive looking, active looking, the event that forced the decision.

Synthesis discipline: within 24 hours, write exact quotes, facts, and commitments, tagged to opportunities. Three interviews with the same unprompted complaint is a pattern worth a bet. One enthusiastic interview is an anecdote. The most common mistake is the word "would" anywhere in the script. This matters to a PM because you will be asked "did you validate it?" and the only honest answer is a list of things people did, not things they said they might do.

Smells to watch for

  • The word "would" anywhere in the script.
  • Pitching in minute two, then "interviewing" for the remaining twenty-eight.
  • Interview notes with adjectives but no exact quotes.
  • "Everyone we talked to loved it" and zero commitments collected.
